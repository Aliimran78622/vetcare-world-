Surgical Management · Cattle Surgery

Caesarean section saves the lives of cows and calves when normal delivery is impossible — but like any major surgery, it carries risks. Uterine tears, haemorrhage, infection and adhesions are the most common complications, and how the uterus is repaired decides whether the cow recovers, breeds again and stays productive.

This guide covers the complications a veterinarian may meet during and after a caesarean section in cattle, the techniques used to repair the uterus, step-by-step postoperative management, and the field measures that prevent problems in the first place.

Important: All surgical procedures must be performed by a qualified veterinarian under proper anaesthesia and aseptic conditions. Never attempt surgery on a cow yourself — incorrect technique causes suffering, infection and death.

Common Caesarean Section Complications

Complications can appear during surgery or in the days after it. The veterinarian must watch for all of them, because a small problem treated early rarely becomes a large one.

  1. 1
    Haemorrhage (bleeding)(Bleeding from uterine vessels or the incision — controlled by ligation, pressure and, when needed, intravenous fluids)
  2. 2
    Uterine tears(The uterus may tear during a difficult delivery, forced traction or calf extraction — tears must be found and repaired before closure)
  3. 3
    Infection and metritis(A contaminated uterus becomes infected — fever, foul-smelling discharge and loss of appetite in the days after surgery)
  4. 4
    Adhesions(Fibrous bands stick the uterus to the omentum or abdominal wall — they cause pain, colic and reduced fertility)
  5. 5
    Retained fetal membranes(Membranes not expelled within 12–24 hours raise the risk of metritis and delayed uterine recovery)
  6. 6
    Wound breakdown (dehiscence)(The abdominal wound opens — caused by infection, strain, poor closure or the cow lying on a dirty floor)

Uterine Repair — Step by Step

Good uterine repair is the heart of a successful caesarean section. The uterus is a large, thin-walled, contaminated organ — it must be handled gently, cleaned and closed in two inverting layers so that no raw tissue or suture is exposed to the abdomen.

  1. 1
    Exteriorise and inspect the uterus(Bring the uterus out onto a sterile drape and examine the whole organ — including the horns — for tears and bruising)
  2. 2
    Remove membranes and clots(Gently remove fetal membranes and blood clots — never pull hard, or the uterus tears further)
  3. 3
    Lavage the uterus(Flush with warm sterile saline to remove debris and reduce contamination)
  4. 4
    Close in two layers(First layer: Cushing (inverting) pattern with absorbable suture. Second layer: Lembert (seromuscular) pattern over the first)
  5. 5
    Check for leaks(Gently press the uterus and watch the suture line — any leak must be oversewn before the uterus goes back)
  6. 6
    Replace the uterus and close the abdomen(Return the uterus to its normal position, check the ovaries and abdomen, then close the abdominal wall in layers)
  7. 7
    Record the repair(Note any tears, the suture material used, drugs given and the cow's condition — the record guides follow-up care)
⚠ Repair Tips:
  • Always use absorbable suture for the uterus — non-absorbable suture causes chronic irritation and adhesions.
  • Keep the uterus moist with warm sterile saline while it is outside the abdomen.
  • Never let uterine contents spill into the abdomen — pack the area with sterile drapes first.
  • Work quickly but gently — long surgery and rough handling increase adhesions.
  • If a tear is too large or the uterus is badly damaged, the veterinarian may need to remove the uterus (hysterectomy) to save the cow's life.

Postoperative Management

Care after surgery decides the outcome as much as the surgery itself. The first 7 to 10 days are the critical period — most complications appear in this window.

  1. 1
    Monitor temperature, appetite and milk twice daily(Fever above 39.5°C, going off feed or a sudden milk drop are early warning signs)
  2. 2
    Antibiotics and pain relief as prescribed by a veterinarian(Complete the full course — stopping early allows infection to return)
  3. 3
    Check the wound daily(Look for swelling, heat, discharge or open stitches — report any change to the veterinarian)
  4. 4
    Encourage standing and walking(Offer soft palatable feed and clean water close by — movement improves circulation and gut function)
  5. 5
    Care for the calf(Colostrum within the first 2 hours, navel disinfection, and a warm dry bed — the calf needs attention too)
  6. 6
    Call the veterinarian at once(Fever, off-feed, foul discharge, wound opening or a cow that will not stand are emergencies)

Prevention of C-Section Complications

Most complications are preventable. Good decisions before and during calving protect the cow far better than any treatment after surgery.

⚠ Prevention Tips:
  • Decide on a caesarean section early — a cow exhausted by hours of forced traction has far more complications.
  • Choose sires matched to the cow's size — oversized calves are the leading cause of dystocia and C-sections.
  • Keep the calving area clean, dry and well bedded, with fresh water always available.
  • Have a trained attendant present at calving and a veterinarian's help arranged in advance.
  • Feed correctly in late pregnancy — avoid both obesity and mineral deficiency, which weaken uterine contractions.
  • Never use excessive force to pull a calf — traction tears the uterus and the calf.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the most common complications after a caesarean section in cattle?

The most common complications are haemorrhage, uterine tears, infection and metritis, retained fetal membranes, adhesions between the uterus and the abdominal wall, and breakdown of the abdominal wound. Early recognition and prompt veterinary treatment decide whether the cow recovers fully and breeds again.

How is a torn uterus repaired during a caesarean section?

The veterinarian exteriorises the uterus, inspects the whole organ for tears, removes membranes and clots, and lavages the uterus with warm sterile saline. Tears are closed with absorbable suture in two inverting layers — a Cushing pattern followed by a Lembert pattern — and the repair is checked for leaks before the uterus is returned to the abdomen.

Why is the uterus closed in two layers?

The first layer, a Cushing (inverting) pattern, turns the cut edges inward so no raw tissue is exposed. The second layer, a Lembert (seromuscular) pattern, buries the first layer and creates a watertight seal. Two layers prevent uterine contents leaking into the abdomen and greatly reduce adhesions and infection.

What causes adhesions after a caesarean section in cows?

Adhesions form when damaged or contaminated tissue heals by sticking to nearby organs — usually the uterus to the omentum or abdominal wall. The main causes are rough handling of the uterus, contamination with uterine contents, bleeding inside the abdomen, infection and long surgery times. Gentle technique, good haemostasis and a clean two-layer closure reduce the risk.

How long does a cow take to recover after a caesarean section?

Most cows stand and eat within hours of surgery and the abdominal wound heals in about 10 to 14 days. Full recovery of the uterus takes 4 to 6 weeks. Breeding should wait until the veterinarian confirms by examination that the uterus has involuted normally, usually after at least 60 days.

How do I know if my cow has a problem after a C-section?

Watch for fever, loss of appetite, less milk, foul-smelling discharge from the vulva, swelling or discharge from the wound, and a cow that stays lying down or grinds her teeth. Any of these signs means call your veterinarian at once — early treatment saves the cow.

Can a cow get pregnant again after a C-section?

Yes, most cows breed normally again after a caesarean section if the uterus was repaired well and no serious infection or adhesions developed. Wait until the veterinarian checks the uterus, usually after 60 days, before breeding. Cows with severe adhesions may have reduced fertility.

Key Takeaway

Caesarean section complications are common but manageable: recognise haemorrhage, tears, infection and adhesions early, repair the uterus in two clean inverting layers, and give careful postoperative care for at least 10 days. Prevention — timely decisions, correct sire selection and clean calving — protects the cow better than any treatment. Follow current local veterinary authority guidance for surgical and drug protocols.
